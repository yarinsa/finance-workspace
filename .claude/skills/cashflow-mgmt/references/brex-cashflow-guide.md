## What cash flow management is

- Tracking the money coming in and measuring it against what goes out.
- **Inflows:** sales, investments, loans. **Outflows:** expenses, repayments, purchases.
- **Positive** cash flow means inflows > outflows. **Negative** means outflows > inflows.

**Household translation:** inflows are salary plus transfers in; outflows are rent, mortgage, loans and card bills. Measure them per account and per date, not just per month.

## Three categories

1. **Operating:** core operations (revenue minus operating costs: suppliers, wages, rent, utilities). The main sustainability indicator.
2. **Investing:** buying or selling long-term assets. Negative often signals growth.
3. **Financing:** transactions with owners and creditors (loans, debt repayment, equity, dividends).

**Household translation:** operating is salary vs living costs. Investing is study-fund/pension deposits and big purchases. Financing is mortgage and loan payments plus overdraft use. A household can look fine on operating and still go negative once financing is included.

## Why it matters (as cited)

- "40% of small businesses had cash flow problems" (Intuit, Jan 2025).
- Businesses that manage cash flow well have roughly **3× higher survival rates** (JPMorgan Chase Institute).
- Poor management causes late payments, missed bulk discounts and broken forecasts.

## Benefits

- Better negotiating position: early-payment discounts of **2–5%**, preferred-customer status.
- More resilience in downturns.
- Lower financing costs, because consistent positive flow gets better rates.
- Agility to act on time-limited opportunities.

**Household translation:** a steady positive balance means no overdraft interest (~10–11% here) and better terms when renegotiating loans or the overdraft limit.

## Techniques

1. **Forecast:** historical patterns and seasonality, trend analysis, modelling software with scenario and sensitivity analysis.
2. **Audit:** track inflows and outflows weekly, monthly or quarterly, and compare forecast with actuals.
3. **Automate bill payments:** fewer errors and fewer late fees.
4. **Optimize payment schedules:** negotiate longer terms, take early-payment discounts, get volume discounts.
5. **Strengthen collections:** automated invoicing, automated follow-ups on overdue payments, payment tracking.

**Household translation:**
1. `forecast.py` plus a per-account balance-by-date view.
2. A monthly review: did the forecast match what actually happened?
3. Standing orders (הו"ק), dated after salary arrives.
4. Move standing-order and card billing dates to after payday.
5. Make sure incoming transfers (salary, family) arrive on a fixed date and into the right account.

## Common challenges → solutions

| Challenge | Brex's solutions |
|---|---|
| Late payments / overdue invoices | Automated invoicing and reminders; early-payment incentive (**2–3% for payment within 10 days**); upfront deposits; digital payment options |
| Unforeseen expenses / downturns | Cash reserve covering **3–6 months of operating expenses**; diversify revenue; flexible supplier terms; preventive maintenance schedules |
| Poor budgeting / forecasting | Multi-year historical analysis; automated variance flagging; best / worst / likely scenarios; monthly budget reviews |
| Bad collection processes | Automate; multiple payment methods; standard follow-up sequence; review aging reports |
| Unclear payment terms | Clear written terms; talk them through; tailor terms to client history; incentives and consequences |

**Household translation:** the reserve rule is the one that applies directly: 3–6 months of fixed costs in liquid savings. Overdraft headroom doesn't count as a reserve. "Scenarios" here means modelling with vs without an income stream (e.g. a partner's contribution stopping). The rest is mostly n/a for households.

## Using a healthy surplus (as listed)

Bulk purchasing for volume discounts, technology that cuts long-term costs, expansion, R&D.

**Household translation:** mostly n/a. The closest equivalents are building the reserve first, then paying down debt that costs more than the reserve earns.
